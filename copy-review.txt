SLIDE 1 · THE ISSUE (title card + issue)
==============================================================================
MGT 402 · Peer Case 1 · Sliver 1A
Paper Rent
How well did Medical Properties Trust’s reported rent and earnings reflect its tenants’ ability to pay?
Annie Wang · Rachel Rosenberg · Shane Haynes
[visual] buildings: MPW, OHI, SBRA; token labels: loan / rent
[title beat 2 caption] The allegation (Viceroy Research, Jan 26, 2023): MPW lends to its tenants, and the money comes back as rent.

How well did reported rent and earnings reflect tenants’ ability to pay?
Accrual accounting records rent when it is earned, not when it is paid, so revenue ahead of cash can be normal. The case question is whether MPW’s tenants could pay what it recognized, and whether lending to those same tenants made its earnings look stronger than its economics.
On Jan 26, 2023, short-seller Viceroy Research alleged that MPW was lending tenants the money to pay its rent.

  Income statement: Rent revenue, incl. straight-line rent; Credit-loss provisions; Impairments
  Balance sheet: Straight-line rent receivable; Loans and investments in tenants; Allowance for credit losses
  Cash flow statement: Net income → CFO reconciliation; Straight-line rent deducted as non-cash revenue; Loans to tenants: investing
  Footnotes: Collectability and cash-basis moves; Tenant concentration; Exposure to tenants

  [explainer] Contractual cash rent / Straight-line revenue / Receivable / Yr / Illustrative lease
  With annual escalators, early rent revenue exceeds the cash due, and the gap builds a receivable. It is not an overdue bill, but it is collected only if the tenant lasts.

1. OHI: ROE 11.1% → 6.6% → 9.8%, CFO above net income every year. FY2022 earnings include a $360.0M gain on assets sold, so not all of it is rent.
2. MPW: ROE 10.6% → −6.9% → −38.6% as Steward unwinds; CFO falls every year ($812M → $245M).

SLIDE 4 · EARNINGS VS. CASH
==============================================================================
[badge] Pre-event window: FY2021–FY2023 · Outcome window: FY2024
In FY2022, MPW’s net income beat its cash flow, mostly because of a property-sale gain.
Total accruals = net income − cash flow from operations (CFO). For a landlord they are normally negative: depreciation lowers net income but uses no cash. Large negatives can also mean impairments and reserves, so they are not a sign of strength.

[beat 1] Total accruals (net income − CFO)
  MPW: FY2021 −$154.7M | FY2022 +$164.8M | FY2023 −$1.06B | FY2024 −$2.65B
  OHI: FY2021 −$293.8M | FY2022 −$186.9M | FY2023 −$368.9M | FY2024 −$331.6M
  SBRA: FY2021 −$469.6M | FY2022 −$393.3M | FY2023 −$286.8M | FY2024 −$183.8M
  marker: MPW FY2022 −$371.9M ex-gain*
  labels: Below zero is normal · MPW FY2022: +$164.8M, because net income included a $536.8M gain on property sales · reserves & impairments · * Analytical, not GAAP: accruals as if the gain were excluded.
  A sale gain raises net income but brings in no operating cash: the CFO reconciliation deducts it, and the $2.19B of sale proceeds is investing cash flow.
[beat 2] MPW: net income vs. CFO, FY2021–23
  Net income: FY2021 $657M | FY2022 $904M | FY2023 −$556M
  CFO: FY2021 $812M | FY2022 $739M | FY2023 $506M
  In FY2022, net income rose 37.6% while CFO fell 9.0%.
  How net income becomes CFO · MPW FY2022
    Gain on property sales $536.8M: deducted
    “Straight-line rent revenue and other” $282.5M: non-cash revenue, deducted
    Impairments $268.4M: non-cash expense, added back
  Off-pattern years: MPW FY2022 (positive, sale gain). MPW FY2023–24 and SBRA FY2021–22 (large negatives from reserves, write-offs and impairments). SBRA’s CFO held at $356M → $316M; MPW’s cash fell too.
[beat 3] What CFO cannot show
  Making and collecting loans are investing activities (Class 7). If a tenant pays rent with money its landlord lent it, the rent lands in CFO and the loan in investing, so CFO alone cannot show self-financed rent.
  Illustrative sensitivity, not GAAP CFO: CFO less all loans advanced was $753M → $532M → $256M (reported $812M → $739M → $506M). Loans went to all borrowers, and not every advance funded rent.

SLIDE 6 · VERDICT, COUNTERARGUMENT, FY2024 CHECK
==============================================================================
MPW’s earnings looked stronger than its cash and tenant credit justified.
We agree with the substance of Viceroy’s critique. MPW’s reported earnings presented a stronger picture than its recurring cash generation and tenant credit exposure justified. The evidence supports concern about economic performance; it does not establish fabricated rent or a GAAP violation.

  Signal 1 · Straight-line receivable ÷ revenue — Much of the rent was not yet cash.
    MPW 47–73% vs OHI 14–21%: more of MPW’s recognized rent depended on tenants paying for years to come.
    Limit: an accumulated balance, not overdue rent; lease terms differ.
  Signal 2 · Largest tenant ÷ revenue — One fragile tenant, financed by MPW.
    Steward was more than 10% of revenue and 19–25% of assets, and MPW lent it a further $150M in 2022. OHI’s largest operator was near 10%; SBRA had none that large.
    Limit: MPW gave only a threshold, not the exact revenue share.
  Supporting, not independent: in FY2022 net income rose 37.6% while CFO fell, mostly a sale gain. In FY2023, $82M of revenue arrived as Prospect securities “in lieu of cash.”
  [beat 2] Strongest fact against us
    A Wachtell Lipton review, engaged by the Audit Committee of MPW’s board, found no evidence of round-tripping or fraudulent revenue recognition.
    • MPW moved Steward to cash basis on Dec 31, 2023, after the first shortfall (Sep 2023) and before the bankruptcy.
    • The positive FY2022 accrual came from a sale gain, not from rent.
    Response: This rules out the fraud version of the critique, not the credit risk. Our signals concern whether tenants could pay, not whether revenue was invented, so our position stands in narrower form.
[beat 3] FY2024 outcome check   [badge] Outcome window FY2024
  Steward charges FY2023: $714M
      Reserve of unpaid rent and lease incentives $154M
      Reserve of straight-line rent receivables $224M
      Reserve of unpaid interest $35M
      Impairment: equity investment and other $171M
      Impairment: real estate $100M
      Reserve in MAM partnership $30M
  Steward charges FY2024: $1.56B
      Working capital and other loans $787M
      Investment in Massachusetts partnership $445M
      Real estate $277M
      Equity investment and other $54M
  MPW allowance for credit losses: $96M → $511M (after $1.24B of provisions and $826M written off)
  Steward’s FY2022 revenue share: 27.7% (first disclosed in the FY2024 10-K, after the bankruptcy)
  The outcome matches the pre-event risk. It does not show that GAAP required these losses earlier: estimates change as new information arrives.
